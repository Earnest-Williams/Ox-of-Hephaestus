# Watch routine

How an ordinary day runs, and how work is split between the lead mind and the
crew. Almost all of this is **New**: the installed game has systems, not yet a
day.

## The crew aboard

**Decided.** The ship is designed for a crew of 40, with one main watch and
three skeleton watches. **The actual crew is 16**, on three overlapping 14-hour
shifts:

| Shift | People | Length | Who |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Main shift | 12 | 14 hours | Always includes the captain; most active work happens here |
| Small shift A | 2 | 14 hours | Mostly monitoring; members rotate |
| Small shift B | 2 | 14 hours | Mostly monitoring; members rotate |

**Rotating** means the small shifts are not always the same people: crew
members take turns on them.

**The ship's day is 36 hours.** Each person works one 14-hour shift in it and
has 22 hours off.

**Everyone is perpetually on call.** Off shift is not out of reach: the lead
mind, the captain or a shift-mate can call anyone at any hour, and being up at
an odd hour is normal.

The habitat has 40 cabins, so 16 people have ample room, and the ECLSS runs far
below its 40-person design.

**Runtime today:** the autonomous crew is five specialists and an optional
captain, still on the seven-compartment proving ground. Sixteen autonomous
minds is well beyond what has been run live; the G12 local profile measured 6,
20 and 40 brains, but not as a live crew.

### Example timetable (to confirm)

One way the three 14-hour shifts fit a 36-hour day, in ship hours, so that
someone is always on duty and each handover overlaps by two hours:

| Ship time | Main (12) | A (2) | B (2) | On duty |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| 00:00–02:00 | on | off | on | 14 |
| 02:00–12:00 | on | off | off | 12 |
| 12:00–14:00 | on | on | off | 14 |
| 14:00–24:00 | off | on | off | 2 |
| 24:00–26:00 | off | on | on | 4 |
| 26:00–36:00 | off | off | on | 2 |

Main runs 00:00–14:00, A 12:00–26:00, and B 24:00 through to 02:00 of the next
day. The hours are illustrative; the shape — a long main shift and two small
monitoring shifts covering the rest of the 36-hour day — is decided.

**Units.** Physical rates elsewhere in these chapters ("kg a day", "operating
days", the 730-day endurance) use the standard 24-hour day. The 36-hour ship's
day is a working schedule; it does not change the physics clock.

## The shape of a day

**New**, proposed:

| Time | Lead mind | Crew |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous | Runs every system it reaches; keeps records; holds course | — |
| Shift handovers | Summarises the last shift: changes, open alarms, work due | The incoming shift reads it; the outgoing one leaves notes |
| Rounds (each shift) | Lists rooms nobody has seen and readings it can't get | A walk through assigned compartments, reading local panels |
| Maintenance (main shift) | Schedules jobs from service intervals and conditions; stages power and access | Engineers do the work; receipts close the jobs |
| Meals | Forecasts stock; keeps the galley powered and water safe | Someone cooks; everyone eats |
| Rest | Quiet running; nothing non-essential near occupied cabins | 22 hours off per 36-hour day, on call throughout |
| Calls | Calls off-shift crew when a job needs them, and records each call and response | Anyone may be called at any hour |
| Captain's time | Reports and recommendations; asks for decisions | The captain decides route, burns, freight, risk |

## Who does what

| The lead mind does it | A person does it |
|---|---|
| Anything a working network path reaches and its authority covers | Anything manual, behind a dead or isolated segment, or in a blind spot |
| Setpoints, schedules, valves and breakers on the network | Manual valves and breakers, resets after trips |
| Watching readings and trends | Reading panels on rounds; reporting what they see |
| Planning work and staging it | Doing it, and verifying it |
| Recording everything | Signing acknowledgements and receipts |
| Recommending | Deciding (the captain), or declining (anyone) |

## Standing orders

**New.** The captain's standing orders govern the lead mind between
decisions, for example:

- keep cold-chain cargo in band;
- never open a cargo boundary without a pressure check;
- wake the captain for any injury, fire or breach;
- no burns without the captain's approval.

Standing orders are world records: who set them, when, and what they say.
